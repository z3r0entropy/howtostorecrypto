## What happened

In June 2020, a security researcher exploited an authenticated API key
on Ledger's e-commerce site to extract part of the customer database.
Ledger disclosed the breach in late July 2020, initially describing
~1 million email addresses and ~9,500 records containing fuller
personal information (name, postal address, phone number).

In December 2020, the full database — approximately 272,000 customer
records — was posted to public file-sharing sites. Customers' home
addresses, phone numbers, names, and emails were now in the open.

No seed phrases, private keys, or wallet data were ever exposed. A
hardware wallet's value is precisely that the secret material *never
leaves the device*; the e-commerce database had no business holding
any of it and didn't.

## What followed

The phishing operations that followed are the actual incident, from a
user's perspective. They came in three shapes:

1. **Fake "Ledger Live" updates.** Emails warning of a "security
   incident requiring you to migrate your assets" linked to lookalike
   sites hosting trojaned versions of Ledger Live. Users who pasted
   their 24-word seed into the recovery flow had their wallets emptied
   within minutes.

2. **Fake "replacement hardware."** Some affected customers received
   physical packages — well-presented, addressed correctly — containing
   what appeared to be a replacement Nano. The devices were
   pre-tampered; using them as instructed handed the seed to the
   attacker.

3. **SIM swap and direct extortion.** With names, addresses, and phone
   numbers, attackers had what they needed to attempt SIM-swap attacks
   on associated accounts, and in some cases simply called or messaged
   victims directly with credible threats.

A meaningful number of losses are linked to these campaigns. Exact
figures are hard to pin down because individual victims do not always
report, and reports are not always independently verified — hence
this entry's amount field is zero (the breach itself stole no crypto)
and the downstream damage is described in prose rather than tallied.

## Why it matters here

The Ledger leak is the case that breaks the comforting frame "a hardware
wallet keeps the secret material safe, so nothing else matters." The
*device* did its job. The *vendor's CRM* did not. The result is a
multi-year stream of perfectly targeted social-engineering attempts
against people identifiable, by name and address, as crypto holders.

The defences that worked are mundane and unrelated to which hardware
wallet you own: install software only from official sources, verify
signatures, treat any unsolicited communication about your wallet as
hostile by default, and consider a separate identity (PO box, alias
email, secondary phone) when buying anything that links your real
address to a meaningful crypto holding.
