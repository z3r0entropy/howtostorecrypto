## What happened

On 3 June 2023, holders of Atomic Wallet — a closed-source, multi-chain
self-custody wallet for desktop and mobile — began reporting that their
balances had been completely drained, simultaneously, across multiple
chains. The reports were not isolated. Within hours hundreds of users
had posted matching stories on Reddit, Telegram, and X.

Atomic Wallet's initial statement acknowledged an exploit affecting
"less than 1% of the active app's monthly users" and committed to
ongoing investigation. A detailed public root-cause analysis was never
published. Independent analysts, working from on-chain data, attributed
the laundering pattern to the Lazarus Group — a North Korean
state-affiliated actor with a well-documented history of crypto theft.

The technical root cause remains formally unresolved in public. The
plausible candidates discussed in the security community include:

- a weakness in how seed phrases were generated in early versions of
  the wallet (insufficient entropy / predictable RNG state),
- a compromise of the binary distribution channel (a malicious build
  served to a subset of users),
- credential theft from a centralised user-data system at Atomic that
  effectively held the keys to many wallets.

Atomic Wallet has implied that the higher analyst estimates
overcounted; analysts have published transaction-cluster evidence
behind their figures. No reconciliation has been published.

## Why this entry's status is "disputed"

The incident itself is not in question — too many independent victims
reported coincident draining for the event to be denied. What is in
question is:

1. the total amount lost,
2. the technical cause,
3. whether the affected population was as small as the vendor
   suggested or as large as the analyst estimates imply.

The user-facing lesson does not depend on resolving any of these. A
self-custody software wallet that holds your seed in plaintext (or
recoverable form) on a general-purpose computer is, on a long enough
timeline, a guaranteed attack surface. Whether it's Atomic or the next
one of its category, the architectural problem is the architecture.

## Why it matters here

Atomic Wallet is the case that distinguishes "self-custody" as an
*intention* from "self-custody" as an *architecture*. Holding your
keys yourself doesn't mean much if the software you ask to hold them
for you is a black box that can be compromised once and drain everyone
at the same time.

The defence is not "stop using software wallets." It's: never let the
software *have* the keys. A hardware wallet — or any air-gapped signer
— keeps the key material outside the wallet UI's reach. The UI shows
you the transaction; the signer alone produces the signature; a
compromised UI can mislead you about what you're signing, but cannot
sign without you.
