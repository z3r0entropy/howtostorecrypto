## What happened

On 2 August 2016, attackers drained 119,756 BTC from Bitfinex's hot
wallets. The exchange's published security architecture was a 2-of-3
multisignature setup with BitGo as the second signer — a configuration
that was meant to make a hack of this magnitude impossible without
compromising both Bitfinex and BitGo simultaneously.

It didn't work that way. Bitfinex had asked BitGo to relax the
withdrawal rate-limiting that would otherwise have flagged large or
unusual transfers. Once the attacker controlled the relevant Bitfinex
infrastructure, BitGo's co-signing was effectively automated against
them: the multisig signed every fraudulent withdrawal as if it were
legitimate.

Bitfinex socialised the loss across all customer accounts as a 36%
"haircut" and issued *BFX tokens* representing the debt. Over the
following months, those tokens were either redeemed for USD, swapped
for equity in the parent company iFinex, or paid down. By April 2017
Bitfinex announced that all BFX tokens had been redeemed — meaning the
exchange's customers were made whole in dollar terms, though not in
the BTC they had originally held.

## The unusual ending

In February 2022, US federal agents arrested Ilya Lichtenstein and his
spouse Heather Morgan in Manhattan and announced the seizure of
approximately 94,000 BTC, then valued at ~$3.6 billion — at the time
the largest financial seizure in DOJ history. The case relied on
years of blockchain analysis tracing the stolen coins through mixers,
exchanges, and a long series of laundering attempts.

Lichtenstein pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit money laundering
in 2023 and was sentenced in 2024. Morgan, separately, pleaded guilty
and received a shorter sentence. The recovered BTC is being processed
for return through Bitfinex's recovery program.

## Why it matters here

Bitfinex 2016 is the case that complicates the simple "use multisig"
advice. The exchange *did* use multisig. The problem was the operational
shape of that multisig — both signers were in effectively the same
trust domain. A geographically distributed multisig, with keys held by
independent parties on different infrastructure, is what the marketing
implied. What was actually deployed was a multi-signature lock with
both keys in the same building.

For an individual setting up self-custody multisig, the lesson is
sharper than "more signers good." It's: *make sure your signers can't
all be compromised by one breach.* If two of three keys live on
different devices in the same room, the redundancy is theatrical.
