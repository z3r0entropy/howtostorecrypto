## What happened

By 2013, Mt. Gox — a Tokyo-based exchange originally built by Jed McCaleb
to trade *Magic: The Gathering* cards — handled the majority of global
Bitcoin trading volume. Internally, the company was a mess: a single
developer codebase, no version control for long stretches, a "wallet"
implemented as one large hot wallet, and an accounting system that did
not reliably distinguish company funds from customer funds.

In February 2014 the exchange suspended withdrawals, then stopped trading,
then filed for bankruptcy protection in Japan, declaring a loss of
approximately 850,000 BTC — around 750,000 belonging to customers and
100,000 belonging to the exchange itself.

The publicly stated cause was a long-running exploitation of *transaction
malleability* — a property of pre-SegWit Bitcoin that allowed transaction
IDs to be altered after signing. Subsequent investigations (including
the WizSec analysis) suggested that transaction malleability accounted
for a small share of the losses and that most of the missing coins had
already been gone, drained from hot wallets over a period of years
before the bankruptcy filing made the absence visible.

About 200,000 BTC were later discovered in an old wallet format and
became the basis of the civil rehabilitation process. Distributions to
creditors began in 2024 — a partial return more than a decade after
the fact, paid out at a small fraction of the coins' eventual market
value.

## Why it's the canonical case

Mt. Gox is the original argument for self-custody. It established —
viscerally, in front of a global audience — that "the exchange holds
it for you" means "the exchange's solvency and competence are your
solvency and competence." Every subsequent exchange failure (QuadrigaCX,
Cryptopia, FTX, Celsius) is a variation on the same theme.

The lesson is not *which* exchange to trust. It is that exchange
custody is a category of risk that disappears entirely the moment you
withdraw to keys you control.
