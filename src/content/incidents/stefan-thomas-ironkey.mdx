## What happened

In 2011, Stefan Thomas — then a programmer, later the CTO of Ripple —
made a short animated video called *What is Bitcoin?* It became, for a
while, the canonical explainer. A grateful viewer paid him 7,002 BTC.

He encrypted the wallet file holding those coins on an IronKey USB
drive and wrote the password down on a piece of paper. The paper was
lost. So, eventually, was the memory of the password.

IronKey drives are designed to resist brute force in the most direct
way available: after ten incorrect password attempts, the drive
permanently and irreversibly encrypts its contents. By the time the
NYT covered the story in January 2021, Thomas had used eight of his
ten attempts. Two remained.

At Bitcoin's peak in late 2021 the wallet was worth roughly $240
million. As of public reporting, Thomas has not yet attempted the
remaining tries — sensibly, given the asymmetric downside of a wrong
guess — and has at various points discussed working with hardware
security teams who claim to be able to extract the encrypted contents
without triggering the wipe.

## Why this is in the incidents corpus

Stefan Thomas's case isn't a hack. Nobody stole from him. The category
of loss is *self-inflicted* — the most common cause of permanent loss
in self-custody, more common than any external attack, and the
category about which the rest of this site has the most to say.

His specific failure mode is *single-medium, single-location, no
backup of the encryption key*. The IronKey was a fine choice for cold
storage. The mistake was treating the IronKey password as if it were
itself stored securely, when in fact it was stored on a piece of paper
that could be lost — the same way a seed phrase can be lost — without
any redundancy.

## Why it matters here

In 2011, the BIP-39 standard didn't exist yet. The whole ergonomic
infrastructure for cold storage — twelve to twenty-four-word
mnemonics, deterministic wallets, hardware signers with screens —
was either not yet invented or not yet mainstream. Thomas's setup was
a reasonable answer to the storage problem of *that* era.

The shape of the loss, however, is identical to what still happens
today. Replace "IronKey password" with "BIP-39 passphrase" or "wallet
encryption password" and the failure mode is the same. The fix is the
same too: the secret you actually need to remember must be backed up
on the same level of medium as the seed itself — at least two
locations, at least one of them not in your head.

Human memory is the least reliable storage device ever invented.
Anything that depends on a single human's recall is a single point of
failure that *will* fail, given enough years.
